Dune

by Frank Herbert

Fear is the mind-killer.

What it's about

Paul Atreides arrives on the desert planet Arrakis as rival powers compete for control of a crucial resource. His family has been handed the most dangerous fief in the empire, and a quiet betrayal is already underway.

What begins as political intrigue becomes a story of survival, prophecy and the slow reshaping of a young man into something much larger than himself.

Why people are reading it

A new generation has met the story through film and games, and keeps finding that the book is stranger, slower and more philosophical than the adaptations suggest.

Readers talk about the appendices, the invented terms and the feeling of being dropped into a world that does not explain itself.

Why it matters

Its world links ecology, empire, religion and the dangers of charismatic leadership. It helped define what a science fiction world could be: layered, lived in and political.

It is also a warning: the hero is not always the answer, and the people who follow him pay for it.

About the author

Frank Herbert (1920–1986) worked as a journalist before turning to fiction. Research into sand dune stabilisation in Oregon sparked the idea for Arrakis.

He wrote five sequels, each questioning the myth of the previous one.
